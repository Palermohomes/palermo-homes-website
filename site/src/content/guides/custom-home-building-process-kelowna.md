Everything that happens when you build a custom home in Kelowna, from first consultation to move-in. Each of the 8 phases explained in plain English: what happens, how long it takes, who is involved, and what you as the client are responsible for.

Published October 2026 · By [Jas Sidhu](/about/jas-sidhu/), Owner of Palermo Homes · Custom Home Builder in West Kelowna, BC

Building a custom home in Kelowna moves through 8 distinct phases over 14 to 20 months: initial consultation, lot evaluation, design, permits, site prep and foundation, framing and envelope, mechanical and interior finishing, and final inspections and occupancy. Each phase has a typical duration, a defined set of professionals involved, and specific things the client is responsible for. Understanding the sequence before you start prevents the two most common mistakes: starting with the wrong lot and starting design before scope is clear.

A completed custom home on Cabernet Way, West Kelowna. The full process from first consultation to this photograph ran approximately 18 months.

Most first-time custom home clients walk into the process with a general idea of the finished product and almost no picture of what happens between first conversation and move-in. The gap between the two is what makes custom home building feel opaque. This guide pulls that gap apart into 8 sequential phases that happen on every build, in the same order, with similar durations across similar projects.

The phases are a sequence, not a checklist to rush through. Each one has decisions that lock the next one in place. Shortcutting earlier phases almost always creates problems in later phases that cost more to fix than the time saved up front.

## The 8 phases at a glance

| Phase | Typical duration | What locks at the end |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Initial consultation and feasibility | 2 to 4 weeks | Decision to proceed, builder selected |
| 2. Lot evaluation and site study | 2 to 4 weeks (if lot owned) | Buildable envelope, site constraints identified |
| 3. Design and architectural development | 3 to 6 months | Final construction drawings |
| 4. Permits and approvals | 6 weeks to 3 months | Building permit in-hand |
| 5. Site preparation and foundation | 6 to 10 weeks | Foundation poured and backfilled |
| 6. Framing and envelope | 10 to 18 weeks | Weather-tight shell |
| 7. Mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and interior finishing | 24 to 40 weeks | Substantially complete home |
| 8. Final inspections, deficiencies, and occupancy | 4 to 8 weeks | Occupancy permit, keys handed over |

## Phase 1: Initial consultation and feasibility

Phase 1

### What this phase actually looks like

2 to 4 weeks

The first conversation is a 60 to 90 minute meeting with the builder, usually in person at their office or on your lot if you own one. The topics covered are your vision, your lot or lot-shopping status, your budget range, your timeline target, and your style direction.

A good builder uses this meeting to assess whether the project is realistic for the budget, flag any obvious lot or scope constraints, and give you a candid first opinion on cost range. If both sides want to move forward, a feasibility study or design agreement follows.

Who is involved

Builder (owner or senior PM), you

What the client delivers

Lot information (if owned), rough budget range, must-have list

## Phase 2: Lot evaluation and site study

Phase 2

### Understanding what the lot can actually support

2 to 4 weeks (if lot owned); longer if actively lot hunting

Before any serious design work, the lot needs to be evaluated for buildability. The builder walks the site, reviews zoning and setbacks, assesses slope and access, and identifies anything that will drive cost or constrain design.

If the lot has not been purchased yet, this phase can run as pre-purchase walk-throughs on candidate lots. The 90 minutes spent on a lot before you offer almost always catches issues that would cost tens of thousands to work around after the deal closes. Hillside status, Development Permit requirements, servicing constraints, soil issues, ridgeline or ravine proximity, and architectural guideline restrictions all get flagged here.

For hillside lots specifically, a geotechnical engineer may be engaged in this phase or at the start of design. See our [hillside builds guide](/guides/hillside-custom-home-kelowna/) for the full breakdown of what sloped lots add to cost and timeline.

Who is involved

Builder, geotech engineer (if hillside), municipal planning (informational calls)

What the client delivers

Lot title, any existing surveys, any municipal correspondence

## Phase 3: Design and architectural development

Phase 3

### Translating vision into construction drawings

3 to 6 months

Design is the phase most homeowners underestimate. It is also the phase where decisions have the biggest downstream impact on both cost and construction speed. The phase includes initial concept drawings, floor plan options, floor plan refinement and sign-off, architectural detailing (exterior elevations, window placements, roof lines), structural engineering, mechanical/electrical/plumbing layout, and interior finish selections.

The clients who finish design in 3 to 4 months are the ones who arrive with a clear vision and make decisive selections. The clients who take 5 to 6+ months are the ones who explore multiple directions, change scope, or struggle with finish decisions. Both are valid approaches, but the longer path adds design cost and delays construction.

Interior finish selections stall more projects than most people expect. Choosing between three tile options takes an afternoon. Choosing between three thousand takes months. Builders who provide curated allowance ranges keep this phase moving.

Who is involved

Architect or designer, structural engineer, Energy Advisor, interior designer, mechanical consultants, builder as project coordinator

What the client delivers

Timely feedback on options, decisions on finish selections, approvals at each design milestone

## Phase 4: Permits and approvals

Phase 4

### Clearing the municipal review

6 weeks to 3 months

Once design is complete and all consulting work is stamped and ready, the full package is submitted to the municipality for building permit review. For hillside lots or lots in Development Permit areas, a DP review runs separately and often precedes the building permit submission.

Permit review times vary by municipality: 15 business days target in West Kelowna, 2 weeks in Peachland, 21 business days standard in Penticton, 4 to 6 weeks typical in Kelowna. Complete, well-prepared applications clear first review with minor comments only. Incomplete applications or complex files trigger revision cycles that add weeks or months.

For the full fee and process breakdown by municipality, see the [Okanagan permits guide](/guides/custom-home-permits-okanagan/).

Who is involved

Builder (as applicant), municipal plans examiner, BC Housing (for warranty registration), Technical Safety BC (for electrical permits)

What the client delivers

Signatures on application forms, payment of DCCs and permit fees

## Phase 5: Site preparation and foundation

Phase 5

### Breaking ground and establishing the base

6 to 10 weeks

Site prep covers clearing, grading, excavation, and installation of underslab services (water, sewer, drainage, sometimes radon mitigation). On hillside lots this phase includes retaining wall installation and more complex drainage. Foundation work follows: footings, foundation walls, waterproofing, perimeter drainage, and backfill.

This is the first visible phase, which creates a false sense that the project is now moving fast. The reality is that months of preparation lead to a few weeks of ground work, and construction then settles into the longer phases that follow.

Who is involved

Excavation contractor, concrete contractor, surveyor (foundation layout), municipal inspector, plumbing contractor (underslab)

What the client delivers

Nothing specific; the client observes and approves foundation layout before pour

## Phase 6: Framing and envelope

Phase 6

### The shell goes up

10 to 18 weeks

Framing is the phase where the home starts to look like itself. Floors, walls, roof structure, windows, exterior doors, roofing, and exterior sheathing all happen here. By the end of this phase, the shell is weather-tight and the interior is protected from the elements.

Clients often want to make design changes during framing because it is the first phase where spaces feel real. Changes at this point are expensive. Moving a wall after framing requires re-engineering, re-permit review in some cases, and ripple effects through mechanical and electrical layouts. The decisions locked in design phase should stay locked.

Who is involved

Framing crew, structural engineer (site inspections), window installer, roofing contractor, exterior finish crews

What the client delivers

Attend first site walk after framing is complete, confirm no last-minute changes

## Phase 7: Mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and interior finishing

Phase 7

### The longest phase, by far

24 to 40 weeks

This is the longest and most complex phase in the entire build. It covers rough-in of mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems, insulation, drywall, interior trim and millwork, cabinets, flooring, tile, paint, lighting fixtures, plumbing fixtures, appliances, and all exterior finishing (siding, soffits, decks, driveway).

The phase includes many simultaneous trade sequences. Framing of mechanical in one room while drywall finishes in another. Cabinet installation in the kitchen while tile crews work in a bathroom. Good construction management sequences trades so each one has a clear path and no one is waiting on another.

Clients should expect bi-weekly site walks during this phase, weekly or bi-weekly progress photo updates, and periodic selection confirmations as specific finish decisions come up (tile grout colour, hardware specifics, final paint sheen).

Who is involved

Mechanical, electrical, plumbing, insulation, drywall, trim, cabinet, flooring, tile, paint, siding, deck, and landscape trades. The builder coordinates all of them.

What the client delivers

Timely selection confirmations, attendance at scheduled site walks, response to any change requests within agreed timeframes

## Phase 8: Final inspections, deficiencies, and occupancy

Phase 8

### From "substantially complete" to keys in hand

4 to 8 weeks

Once construction is substantially complete, final inspections begin: building, plumbing, electrical, gas, and in some cases fire. Each inspection may surface deficiencies that need to be corrected and re-inspected.

The homeowner walks through the home with the builder for a formal deficiency list review. Most lists have 20 to 60 items on a typical build: paint touch-ups, trim adjustments, hardware tweaks, minor cosmetic issues. The builder resolves these over 2 to 4 weeks. Occupancy permit is issued once the final inspection sign-offs are complete, typically 1 to 2 weeks after the final inspection passes.

Keys are handed over, the Home Warranty becomes active, and the project transitions to post-occupancy support. Palermo Homes maintains a direct communication channel with every client for warranty questions and ongoing support.

Who is involved

Municipal inspectors (building, plumbing, electrical, gas, fire), builder, client, Energy Advisor (final verification), warranty provider

What the client delivers

Participation in deficiency walk, timely decisions on any remaining selections (e.g. landscape plants), final payment

## What happens in parallel across phases

The 8-phase structure above is sequential at the high level but many activities run in parallel inside and across phases. Finish selections continue into early construction. Structural engineering continues during permit review. Trade scheduling begins before site prep finishes. A good project manager maintains a master schedule that keeps every parallel activity moving without creating bottlenecks.

What should not run in parallel: major scope decisions and active construction. Clients who change scope during framing or finishing almost always see weeks of delay and significant change-order cost. The design phase is the place to make scope decisions.

## What a client actually does across all 8 phases

Over 14 to 20 months, the client's time commitment averages 2 to 6 hours per week during active phases, concentrated in the design phase and in site walks during construction. Specifically:

- **Design phase (3 to 6 months):** 4 to 8 hours per week on finish selections, floor plan reviews, meetings with the design team
- **Permit phase:** Minimal client involvement; the builder manages submission and response to municipal comments
- **Site prep and foundation:** 1 to 2 site visits, otherwise observational
- **Framing and envelope:** First site walk after framing complete; otherwise observe photo updates
- **Finishing (longest phase):** Bi-weekly site walks, selection confirmations as needed
- **Final phase:** Deficiency walk, final payment, move-in

A well-run custom home does not require the client to be on-site daily or to manage trades directly. That is the builder's job. The client's job is to make clear decisions in design, show up for the key milestones, and let the builder run the construction.

> "The best custom home clients are the ones who understand what phase we are in and make phase-appropriate decisions. Design decisions during design. Finish decisions before trades arrive. Nothing after framing except colour and hardware tweaks. The projects that go over budget are almost always the ones where the client treats construction as an extension of design." Jas Sidhu, Owner, Palermo Homes

## Common mistakes to avoid

1. **Starting design before the lot is confirmed.** Design decisions depend on the specific lot. Design developed for one lot rarely transfers cleanly to another.
2. **Rushing design to meet a move-in date.** Shortcutting design almost always costs more in change orders than it saves in weeks.
3. **Submitting an incomplete permit application.** Permit review restarts on affected sections every time revisions are required.
4. **Making major scope changes during construction.** Each change during framing adds 2 to 6 weeks and significant cost.
5. **Choosing a builder based on the lowest bid.** Final cost is far more about builder capability than initial quote. See the [builder selection guide](/guides/how-to-choose-custom-home-builder-kelowna/).

## What this looks like on a real Kelowna build

Our [Cabernet Way project](/project/cabernet-2/) ran the full 8-phase process over approximately 18 months. Initial consultation and lot evaluation took 3 weeks. Design ran 4 months. Permits came through in 10 weeks. Site prep, foundation, framing, and finishing ran 11 months of active construction. Final inspections and deficiency work took 6 weeks. Total: approximately 18 months from first meeting to keys-in-hand.

This guide reflects the current process, timelines, and requirements for custom home building in the Central Okanagan as of 2026. Municipal review times, trade availability, and specific requirements change. Verify current specifics with any builder you interview.
