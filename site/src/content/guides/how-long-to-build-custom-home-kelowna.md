A realistic month-by-month timeline for designing, permitting, and building a custom home in Kelowna and the Okanagan, plus the factors that extend or compress each phase.

Published October 2026 · By [Jas Sidhu](/about/jas-sidhu/), Owner of Palermo Homes · Custom Home Builder in West Kelowna, BC

A typical custom home in Kelowna takes 14 to 20 months from first design meeting to move-in. That breaks down as 3 to 6 months for design, 6 weeks to 3 months for permits (varies by municipality), 8 to 14 months for construction, and 1 to 2 months for deficiency work and occupancy. Hillside lots, larger homes over 5,000 square feet, and complex custom features extend the timeline. Flat lots with a locked design and a Development Permit-free site finish faster.

Timeline is the question almost every Kelowna custom home client asks first, often before cost. The honest answer involves ranges, not a single number, because every build has its own site conditions, scope, and sequence of decisions. This guide walks through each phase of a Kelowna custom home with realistic durations, where delays actually come from, and how to plan a move-in date that holds up.

## The four phases of a Kelowna custom home

Every custom build in the Okanagan runs through the same four phases. Each one has a typical duration and a set of things that can stretch it.

| Phase | Typical duration | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design | 3 to 6 months | Concept, floor plans, architectural detailing, engineering, finish selections |
| Permits | 6 weeks to 3 months | Building permit application, review, revisions, issuance. Faster in West Kelowna, Peachland, Penticton; variable in Kelowna. |
| Construction | 8 to 14 months | Site prep, foundation, framing, mechanical, finishes, exterior |
| Deficiency and occupancy | 1 to 2 months | Final inspections, deficiency list, occupancy permit, move-in |

Added together, that is a realistic 14 to 20 months. Shorter is possible on a simple build. Longer is common on anything with meaningful site complexity or scope that evolves during design.

## Phase 1: Design (3 to 6 months)

Design is the phase most homeowners underestimate. It is also the phase where decisions have the biggest downstream impact on both cost and construction speed.

### What design actually includes

- Initial consultations and site walk
- Concept drawings and floor plan options
- Floor plan refinement and client sign-off
- Architectural detailing (exterior elevations, window placements, roof lines)
- Structural engineering stamped drawings
- Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing layout
- Interior finish selections (flooring, tile, cabinetry, plumbing fixtures, lighting)

### What extends design

The most common cause of a long design phase is changing direction mid-stream. A client who signs off on a floor plan, then two months later asks to move the kitchen or add a second-floor wing, resets the clock on structural and mechanical work.

Finish selections also stall more projects than most people expect. Choosing between three tile options takes an afternoon. Choosing between three thousand takes months. Builders who provide curated allowance ranges keep this phase moving.

## Phase 2: Permits (6 weeks to 3 months)

Building permit review times vary significantly across the Okanagan. These are the current published targets or best-available estimates by municipality:

- **District of West Kelowna:** 15 business days (about 3 weeks) for a complete single-family or duplex application. [Published municipal target.](https://www.westkelownacity.ca/building-business-development/building-permits-and-inspections/)
- **City of Penticton:** 21 business days (about 4 weeks) standard, or 1 to 14 business days for eligible applications through the Residential Xpress program. [Published municipal target.](https://www.penticton.ca/business-building/building-permits/building-permit-timelines)
- **District of Peachland:** Approximately 2 weeks per the municipal building department FAQ, first-come first-served, dependent on application completeness. [Published municipal estimate.](https://www.peachland.ca/departments/building-department/building-permit-faqs)
- **City of Kelowna:** No published day-count target. Industry estimates run 4 to 6 weeks for a complete application; complex files take longer. The city operates a Fast Track program for eligible applications.
- **City of Vernon:** No published day-count target. Recent council reporting has flagged permit processing in the "green zone" with no significant delays, with past averages under 4 weeks.
- **District of Lake Country:** No current municipal day-count target published. Historical news coverage has flagged backlogs. Expect to confirm current status directly with the municipality.

These targets assume a complete application. Incomplete submissions, revisions during review, or files that trigger additional department referrals (planning, engineering, environmental) extend the timeline. Properties with foreshore work on Okanagan Lake or Kalamalka Lake add a provincial review that can extend weeks or months beyond municipal timelines.

Hillside lots requiring a Development Permit (DP) review add time on top of the standard building permit timeline. Specific DP durations are not published by most Okanagan municipalities, so plan for additional weeks on any hillside build.

### What extends permits

Revisions during review are the single biggest cause of permit delays. If the municipality requests structural or code changes, that portion of the review restarts. A well-prepared permit set drawn by professionals who build in the specific municipality often usually clears first review with minor comments only.

## Phase 3: Construction (8 to 14 months)

Active construction is the longest phase and the most visible one. For a 3,000 square foot home on a flat lot with straightforward specifications, 8 to 10 months is realistic. A 5,000 square foot home with hillside structural work, custom millwork, imported stone, or complex mechanical systems runs 12 to 14 months.

### Construction stages and timing

- **Site prep and excavation:** 2 to 4 weeks
- **Foundation and underslab mechanical:** 4 to 6 weeks
- **Framing:** 6 to 10 weeks
- **Roof, windows, exterior envelope:** 4 to 8 weeks
- **Mechanical, electrical, plumbing rough-in:** 4 to 6 weeks
- **Insulation and drywall:** 3 to 5 weeks
- **Interior finishes (flooring, tile, cabinets, paint):** 10 to 16 weeks
- **Final mechanical, plumbing, electrical:** 3 to 5 weeks
- **Exterior landscaping, driveway, final site:** 3 to 6 weeks

These stages overlap. Framing finishes before windows are installed. Insulation starts in some rooms while mechanical rough-in continues in others. A good construction schedule sequences trades so that each one has a clear path and no one is waiting on another.

### What extends construction

- **Change orders.** Each major change during construction adds 2 to 6 weeks and often more. Moving a wall, adding a bathroom, upgrading finishes beyond allowance all push the schedule.
- **Weather.** Okanagan winters can delay concrete pours, roofing, and exterior work. A well-planned build schedules weather-sensitive work for the right season.
- **Specialty trade availability.** Custom millwork, heritage-style stonework, and specialized metal fabrication have long lead times in the Okanagan. Ordering early matters.
- **Imported materials.** Italian tile, European windows, specialty appliances. Lead times of 12 to 20 weeks are not uncommon.
- **Hidden site conditions.** Unexpected rock on a hillside lot, poor soil requiring additional engineering, utility conflicts underground.

## Phase 4: Deficiency and occupancy (1 to 2 months)

Once construction is substantially complete, final inspections begin. Building, plumbing, electrical, gas, and in some cases fire inspections all need to pass. Deficiencies identified during inspections get fixed, re-inspected, and signed off.

The deficiency list that the homeowner walks through with the builder usually takes 2 to 4 weeks to resolve. Occupancy permit issuance from the municipality is typically 1 to 2 weeks after the final inspection passes.

## How Kelowna-specific factors change the timeline

### Hillside lots

Hillside builds in neighborhoods like [Upper Mission](/neighborhoods/upper-mission/), [Lakeview Heights](/neighborhoods/lakeview-heights/), [Wilden](/neighborhoods/wilden/), and [Predator Ridge](/neighborhoods/predator-ridge/) add 2 to 4 months to the overall timeline. The extra work includes geotechnical investigation, retaining walls, drainage engineering, Development Permit review, and more complex foundation work.

### Waterfront and lakefront builds

Builds on Okanagan Lake, Kalamalka Lake, or Wood Lake with any foreshore work add provincial review. Riparian area regulations, archaeological assessments, and Transport Canada approvals for anything over the water each add weeks or months.

### Architectural guideline communities

Master-planned communities with design review (Wilden, [Kettle Valley](/neighborhoods/upper-mission/), Upper Mission sub-communities, [McKinley Beach](/neighborhoods/mckinley-beach/)) add 4 to 10 weeks for architectural review before building permit submission. Working with a builder who knows the specific guidelines prevents design bounces.

### Rural and acreage lots

Acreage builds in Lake Country, outer West Kelowna, or beyond municipal boundaries often require septic design, well drilling, hydro service installation, and sometimes road access improvements. Each of these adds weeks and can run in parallel with other design work if planned early.

## What we have seen on real Kelowna projects

On our [Cabernet Way project](/project/cabernet-2/) in West Kelowna, design ran 4 months, permits came through in 10 weeks, construction took 11 months, and deficiency plus occupancy was 6 weeks. Total: approximately 18 months from first meeting to move-in. The hillside site added about 2 months to what a flat-lot build of the same size would have taken.

Our [Malbec Place project](/project/malbec-place-1/) ran faster, approximately 15 months total, because the client arrived with a near-finalized concept, made decisive finish selections, and did not change scope during construction. The lot was also flat with straightforward foundations.

The longest project we have run in recent years went 24 months, driven primarily by three major scope changes during construction and a long-lead imported stone package. None of those delays were surprises in hindsight. Each was a decision the client made, understood, and accepted at the time.

> "The clients who finish on schedule are the ones who make decisions in design and stop making them in construction. Every change during framing is weeks and dollars. We do not discourage changes when they matter, but clients should understand what they cost before they request them." Jas Sidhu, Owner, Palermo Homes

## How to plan a realistic move-in date

Work backward from your target move-in and add buffer:

1. Pick a realistic move-in month. Avoid committing to specific school start dates or wedding events on the same month as projected occupancy.
2. Subtract 18 months as a baseline. That gets you to the "start of design" month.
3. Add 2 to 4 months buffer for anything hillside, complex, or on a tight lot.
4. Start builder conversations 1 to 2 months before design begins.

For a summer 2028 move-in, that means builder conversations by late 2026, design starting by early 2027, and permits in-hand by late 2027.

## How to compress the timeline (safely)

Pushing a Kelowna custom home faster than 14 months is possible but requires giving something up. Realistic ways to save time without cutting corners:

- Start with a near-complete concept rather than exploring options during paid design time
- Lock in finish selections before construction starts, not during
- Choose domestic materials over imported wherever possible
- Build on a flat lot in a non-hillside neighborhood
- Avoid architectural review communities if speed matters more than guidelines
- Submit permits in late fall when queues are shorter

Compressing below 10 months on a true custom home usually means buying a modified spec home instead. There is nothing wrong with that path, but it is a different product than a true custom build.

This guide reflects current permit and construction conditions in the Central Okanagan as of 2026. Municipal review times, trade availability, and material lead times change. Verify current specifics with the relevant municipality and any builder you interview.
